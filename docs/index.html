Advaith Foundation

Education nonprofit · Bangalore, India

What they do

A charitable trust set up in 2004 that pays for the entire schooling, food and housing of children from very poor families in and around Bangalore, free to the family. Its Ankur programme puts those children into small residential homes and sends them to The Samhita Academy, a CBSE school off Bannerghatta Road, where they sit in the same classrooms as children whose parents pay fees; the money comes from donors and company CSR budgets, not from the children.

Inside, day to day. Teachers plan and mark lessons and run after-school tuition, house parents and wardens live with the children and track each one's attendance, health, marks and behaviour largely on paper, and a small office team writes the donor and CSR reports, keeps the trust's compliance filings in order, and handles the visits and paperwork that come with taking in a child.

The scholarship form stops living as a photo

Today each Grade 5 scholarship application to Ankur arrives by post, email or WhatsApp, as a photo or scan of a form with a passport photo, Aadhar copy, birth certificate, caste and income certificate and last report card attached; an administrative staff member opens it, checks the set is complete, reads the declared family income against eligibility, and then — and this last step is a guess, since no online application route exists on the site — copies the details by hand into the list the intake runs from, one applicant at a time, all through the intake window.

With AI she builds and runs a tool that reads each submitted form — scan, WhatsApp photo or emailed PDF — pulls out the child's details and the declared income, checks the five required documents are all there, and drops a completed row into the intake list, flagging anything missing for her to chase; she keeps tuning it herself as the form's fields change each admissions cycle. That is time back for the eligibility judgement itself, which stays hers.

Five minutes open three synthetic Grade 5 applications built to match the real form — one a WhatsApp photo, one a scanned post submission, one an emailed PDF — run the tool live and watch a structured row and a missing-document flag appear for each; then, live, change the declared annual income on one synthetic form and re-run it, so the audience watches the eligibility flag flip because it read the new number, not a cached one.

Legal tech, workplace compliance · Bengaluru, India

What they do

TrustIn sells software and services that help employers deal with workplace harassment and misconduct complaints under India's POSH law. Companies and nonprofits buy it to write their policy, train their staff, take complaints confidentially and run each case through to a decision.

Inside, day to day. The days go on casework and compliance paperwork: writing and updating a policy for each client, running training sessions including in Kannada, staffing a confidential reporting channel and a Telegram bot so an employee can report something without being identified, walking an employer's internal committee step by step through the legal process of judging a complaint, and filing the reports employers are legally required to submit, while a small product team builds the case-management tool the rest of them use.

The case-note draft writes itself before she edits it

Today — and this is inferred from the blog's own byline field, which could default to an admin account rather than confirm who actually drafted each piece — TrustIn's Founder and CEO, Meghana Srinivas, is credited on all six case-note and compliance-explainer posts published between March and September 2026, each one built around reading a full court judgment, a Bombay High Court ruling on IC jurisdiction, a Supreme Court case, and writing up what it means for employers, roughly once a month.

With AI she builds an agent that reads the text of a newly delivered judgment and drafts a first-pass case note in the structure of her last six posts, then she edits every line and decides what the ruling actually means before it goes out under her own name — the research-and-draft afternoon becomes a twenty-minute edit, and that is time back.

Five minutes open a real, public court judgment of the kind the blog already cites, run the agent to produce a draft case note, then swap in a second public judgment live and run it again, so the audience watches the draft change to match the new parties, court and ruling instead of repeating the same template.

Tvarita Collective

Arts and culture · Bengaluru, India

What they do

Tvarita finds traditional Indian folk and classical artists and puts them into paid work. It sells live performances, storytelling shows, art workshops and handmade artist products to schools, colleges, companies, festivals and households, and pays the artists out of what it earns.

Inside, day to day. Two co-founders and a small team spend their days tracking down and verifying artists across dozens of regional art forms, matching the right troupe to an enquiry from a school or a company, quoting and scheduling the event, arranging travel and logistics for performers coming in from villages, documenting each artist's story and tradition in writing and video, and then running the event on the day.

Vidhya Vidhai

Education nonprofit · Chennai, Tamil Nadu, India

What they do

Vidhya Vidhai is a nonprofit that trains head teachers and teachers in government and low-income schools across Tamil Nadu and Puducherry to run their schools better. The schools do not pay: funders such as Wipro Foundation pay for the programmes, and one idea they built, a student house system called Magizh Mutram, was picked up by the Tamil Nadu education department and rolled out across government schools.

Inside, day to day. Around twenty-five to forty staff, split into district field teams (Coimbatore, Nagapattinam, Tirunelveli, Cuddalore, Sivagangai) and central teams for programme design, research, people and culture, growth and communications, spend their days travelling out to schools, running leadership workshops with head teachers, writing up visit notes and filling trackers and reports afterwards, and gathering data across hundreds of schools to show funders what actually changed.

The needs-assessment write-up drafts the two-year plan

Today — and we are guessing at the exact title here, since Vidhya Vidhai's own account only says "our team" — the Programme Design Lead pulls together five separate threads of a new school's needs assessment (classroom observations, teacher interviews, school-leader interactions, student assessments, parent interactions) by hand into a two-academic-year implementation plan, then repeats the same synthesis at baseline and again at endline for every school or cluster the programme takes on.

With AI she builds a drafter that reads the five assessment inputs and produces the implementation plan in the organisation's own structure, and reruns it on the baseline and endline notes to draft what changed; she edits it herself each time a new school's context is different. The plan and the change write-up come back as a draft to edit instead of a blank page to fill — that is time back.

Five minutes open three synthetic assessment notes on screen, clearly labelled "synthetic, for demonstration" — a classroom-observation note, a teacher-interview note, and a parent-interaction note for a made-up school — and run the drafter to produce a one-page plan. Then, live, edit one line in the teacher-interview note, changing a stated weakness from "lesson planning" to "attendance tracking," and rerun it in front of the audience so the plan's priority-actions section visibly changes to match the new note, showing it is reading the actual input, not filling a template.

Zerodha

Retail stockbroking, fintech · Bengaluru, India

What they do

Zerodha is India's largest stockbroker. Ordinary people open an account and buy and sell shares, mutual funds and derivatives through its apps, Kite for trading, Coin for mutual funds and Console for statements, and Zerodha charges a flat fee of up to twenty rupees per order, with nothing charged on ordinary share purchases.

Inside, day to day. It serves more than ten million customers with only about a thousand staff and no sales team, no advertising and no sales targets, so most of the work is support and back office: answering an enormous daily volume of customer tickets and phone calls, checking identity documents to open new accounts, and doing the daily regulatory filings the exchanges and the regulator demand, alongside a famously small in-house engineering team (reported at around thirty-six people) that builds the trading platforms and a team that writes free investor-education material called Varsity.

Zuva Life

Midlife transitions, coaching · Bengaluru, India

What they do

Zuva Life runs programmes for people in midlife and beyond who are working out what they do next, covering longer careers, new kinds of work, money, learning and staying connected. It sells workshops, small-group cohort journeys, retreats and one-to-one sessions, mostly through employers, wellness and longevity companies, career platforms and coaches rather than straight to individuals.

Inside, day to day. A very small team around founder Vandana Srivastava, with a co-founder, an associate, a brand designer and a bench of outside coaches and advisors, spends its days designing session content and exercises, recruiting and then running each cohort, holding one-to-one conversations with participants, writing brand and social content, and pitching the programme to HR teams and wellness partners who will bring a group.
